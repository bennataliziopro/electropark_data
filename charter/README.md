# Built here, hired here.

ElectroPark is an electric-vehicle storage and charging facility about to go up in Ontario. While it's being built, there will be hundreds of people on that ground. After that, there will be people working here every day, and neighbours who see it from their windows.

This charter says what we're committing to for those people. It's written before the build starts, so it can be held against us during.

## Why we start with people

An EV charging site under a roof of solar panels is easy to defend on environmental grounds. A little too easy, frankly.

**The green credit isn't ours.** Ontario's grid is already close to carbon-free. Saying our site "decarbonizes mobility" would mean taking credit for the grid's work. What's actually ours is the concrete we pour and the way we'll run this place for the next thirty years.

**The real question gets asked somewhere else.** At the council meeting, and at the coffee shop across the road, nobody will ask about panel yields. They'll ask who gets to work here, whether local firms get a shot, and whether Indigenous communities heard from us before the permits or after.

**That's where we can be better.** Everyone announces decarbonization. Far fewer are willing to publish a local hiring rate and be audited on it. That's the gap we're choosing, because it can be checked.

## What we're committing to

Five commitments. Each one starts from a Canadian or Ontario rule that already exists, and goes a step further.

### 01. Build here, hire here

Whoever clears the lanes in January, and whoever fixes a charger on a Sunday night, will probably live twenty minutes from here. Those jobs aren't going anywhere. The only question is who gets them.

Ontario was the first province to write community benefits into law, for public infrastructure projects. We're not a public project, so nothing obliges us. We're taking the standard anyway, because it's known here and local partners already know how to work with it.

> **In practice** — A community benefits agreement signed with the municipality and local employment agencies before the first shovel goes in the ground. Apprentices sponsored on site. A local employment report presented to council every year, numbers included, in the years we fall short as well.

### 02. Relationship first, contract second

In Ontario, the duty to consult Indigenous communities belongs to the Crown. In practice, the real work is handed to the proponent: inform, meet, listen, answer, and keep a record. We start at the design stage. A relationship struck up on the day you need a signature shows, and fairly so.

We follow how things are done here rather than an imported protocol: a land acknowledgement at every public milestone, conversations with Elders and knowledge keepers, tenders open to Indigenous businesses from the construction phase on. September 30, the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, will be observed on site. Ontario law doesn't require that of employers like us, which is exactly what makes the gesture mean something.

> **In practice** — A request for a duty-to-consult assessment filed with the province before any permit application. A dated, readable consultation record, showing the questions asked and the answers given. A training partnership with an Indigenous employment services organization in the region.

### 03. Pay in the posting, credentials recognized

Since January 2026, Ontario requires the salary range in the job ad, bans asking for Canadian work experience, requires employers to say when AI screens applications, and to answer within 45 days anyone who came in for an interview.

We're not going to pretend that's a burden. A good share of Ontario's workforce trained somewhere else, and the habit of setting those applications aside would cost us capable people. A diploma earned in Manila, Lagos or Bucharest counts here.

> **In practice** — Compliant postings from the first campaign, and an answer to anyone we interview faster than the law asks: 21 days, instead of the 45 allowed. When we put a corrective measure in place, we write it as a special program under the Ontario Human Rights Code, tracked and documented, never as a quota in disguise.

### 04. A site nobody is turned away from

Accessibility is settled on the drawings, not on opening day. A charger mounted too high, a sidewalk that stops, an app a screen reader can't handle: that gets fixed with construction work, or it doesn't get fixed at all. We treat the provincial deadline as a project milestone, same as the slab.

> **In practice** — The offer of an accommodation made at the three hiring moments the law sets out. Spaces and chargers genuinely usable from a wheelchair, not just counted. Continuous pedestrian routes. An app and a portal that meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA. An accessibility plan published online, readable by anyone.

### 05. What we buy commits us too

The chargers, the panels, the monitoring electronics: all of it comes from global supply chains where forced labour is documented. A charter that stops at the site gate leaves out the most exposed part of the project.

Opening our purchasing to Indigenous, local and minority-owned businesses isn't charity: a contract supports a whole team, where a job supports one person. We count by contract value, not by number of suppliers, because the second way of counting is easy to game.

> **In practice** — A clause banning forced labour and child labour in every goods contract. A share of our spend set aside for verified suppliers, with the verification source named. A purchasing review every quarter during construction.

## The numbers we're holding ourselves to

A commitment without a number is worth nothing, and a number without a date isn't worth much more. Here are both. These targets are proposed by the project team and still have to be settled with the municipality and the client: we'd rather put forward numbers you can argue with than leave the boxes empty. Representation targets rest on voluntary self-identification by employees, never on a judgment of ours.

| What we're aiming for | By when | How you'll know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **40% of construction hours** worked by people living in the host municipality or a neighbouring one | End of construction | Hours tracked by postal code, aggregated, presented to council |
| **8 apprentices** sponsored over the course of the build | End of construction | Apprenticeship agreements registered with the province |
| **70% of operating jobs** filled locally | 18 months after opening | Annual employment report |
| **5% of our contract value** awarded to verified Indigenous businesses | First full fiscal year | Contract value cross-checked against the federal Indigenous Business Directory |
| **15% of our contract value** awarded to local, Indigenous or certified diverse suppliers | First full fiscal year | Certification on file with each purchase |
| **Gender parity and 30%** from under-represented groups in the site leadership team | 5 years | Voluntary self-identification, results published in aggregate |
| **An answer within 21 days** to anyone we interview, where the law allows 45 | From the first campaign | Timestamps kept three years, as the law requires |
| **One accessible charging bay per level**, above the regulatory minimum | Opening | Permit drawings and as-built survey |
| **100% of goods contracts** carrying the forced labour and child labour clause | First tender | Standard clause published with the tender documents |
| **100% of contractors** holding a health and safety certification recognized in Ontario | Bid qualification | Certificates attached to the bid file |
| **Zero lost-time injuries** | Every year | Declarations to the provincial board, published in our report |
| **Social report published within four months** of year-end | Every year | Posted online, including the targets we miss |

## The building and the power

On a grid that's already clean, a parking structure's footprint sits somewhere else: in what we pour, and in the hour we draw power.

**The carbon is already spent on opening day.** Most of a multi-storey parking structure's footprint goes into concrete and steel, before the first vehicle arrives. No amount of solar production catches that up afterwards. So we set a carbon ceiling in the specification, we check it against the digital model the project already calls for, and we ask every bidder for a lower-carbon variant, costed next to their base offer. They know the materials. We don't.

**The real issue is peak demand.** Producing a lot over the year counts for nothing if everyone charges at the same moment. We spread power across the chargers according to when vehicles are due out, we size the battery to shave peaks and ride through an outage, and we publish a self-consumption rate measured at the meter rather than a brochure figure.

**And afterwards.** We're designing the building so it can be something else in twenty years, with a minimum share of recycled aggregate in the structure. Battery end-of-life is part of the subject, not just battery commissioning: the Canadian sector is building a recycling chain, and we'll plug into it rather than invent our own.

## Safety, on both sides of opening day

This is where the law is hardest during construction, and least equipped once the site is running.

**During construction.** Ontario law names someone who cannot pass the buck: whoever holds the role of *constructor* answers for every company on the project complying with the Act. Notice of project filed, joint health and safety committee set up, written emergency procedures reviewed with it, competent supervision at all times. This isn't paperwork. It's what gets people home at night.

**What we require of contractors.** Every bidding company must hold a health and safety management certification recognized in Ontario, and keep it for the length of the contract. We're matching what Ontario's public buyers already ask for.

**Once the site is open.** Several hundred lithium-ion batteries in an enclosed, multi-storey building is not an ordinary parkade. A battery fire burns longer, puts out badly, and takes a great deal of water. The codes haven't caught up with this use yet, so we go past them: a safety plan built with the municipal fire service, a joint exercise before opening, and named training for the operating teams.

## How you'll be able to check

None of these commitments are verified by us. Each one goes through an official filing, an external audit, or a publication.

| Program | What gets looked at, and by whom | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PAIR** | Our relationships with Indigenous communities: leadership decisions, employment, business development, community relations. An Indigenous jury runs the interviews itself. | Voluntary certification |
| **50-30 Challenge** | Our public commitment on leadership diversity, with a progress report expected. | Voluntary commitment |
| **CAMSC** | Real ownership of our suppliers by Indigenous or minority owners. | Supplier certification |
| **Accessibility** | A compliance report certified by a senior officer, filed on the provincial portal and renewed every three years. | Legal obligation |
| **Health and safety** | Our contractors' management systems, audited against a standard recognized in Ontario. | Contractual requirement |
| **Management systems** | Our own environmental, energy and occupational health and safety systems, certifiable by an accredited body. | Voluntary certification |

A word on wording: we say "charter aligned with the international social responsibility guidance", and never "certified charter". That guidance leads to no certification at all. The management systems above do.

## Who answers for what

- **A named owner for each commitment**, appointed the day the charter is adopted, not six months later.
- **Few indicators, collected once a year** on an announced date, rather than a dashboard nobody fills in after the third month.
- **Dated targets with a baseline**, so a gap is a fact rather than a debate.
- **A public social report every year**, published in the years we fall short too.
- **A liaison committee** with the municipality, the Indigenous communities concerned, and local employment agencies.
- **A purchasing review every quarter** during construction, then once a year in operation.

## The rules this is built on

- **Ontario Human Rights Code** — non-discrimination in employment, and the framework for special programs. [Ontario Human Rights Commission](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/your-guide-special-programs-and-human-rights-code)
- **Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act, 2005** — employment, information and public-space design standards, and a compliance report every three years.
- **Employment Standards Act, 2000** — job posting rules in force since January 1, 2026.
- **Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982** — Aboriginal and treaty rights, and the duty to consult. [Ontario](https://www.ontario.ca/page/environmental-assessments-consulting-indigenous-communities)
- **Provincial Planning Statement, 2024** — early engagement with Indigenous communities in land use planning.
- **Infrastructure for Jobs and Prosperity Act, 2015** — Ontario's definition of community benefits. [Legislative Assembly of Ontario](https://www.ola.org/en/legislative-business/bills/parliament-41/session-1/bill-6)
- **Building Opportunities in the Skilled Trades Act, 2021** — apprenticeship and the journeyperson-to-apprentice ratio. [Skilled Trades Ontario](https://www.skilledtradesontario.ca/apprenticeship/sponsor-an-apprentice/)
- **Occupational Health and Safety Act and O. Reg. 213/91** — construction projects and the constructor's duties. [Ontario](https://www.ontario.ca/page/hazards-construction-sector)
- **Fighting Against Forced Labour and Child Labour in Supply Chains Act** — reporting obligations. [Justice Laws Canada](https://laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-10.6/page-1.html)
- **Federal minimum 5% Indigenous procurement target** — the figure we're taking on voluntarily. [Indigenous Services Canada](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1691786841904/1691786863431)

# Charte RSE — ElectroPark Ontario

**Version française.** Les deux versions sont identiques sur le fond ; en cas d'écart, la version anglaise fait foi auprès du conseil municipal.

## Bâtir ici, avec ceux d'ici.

ElectroPark, c'est un parc de stockage et de recharge de véhicules électriques qui va sortir de terre en Ontario. Pendant la construction, il y aura des centaines de personnes sur ce terrain. Ensuite, il y aura des gens qui y travailleront tous les jours, et des voisins qui le verront depuis chez eux. Cette charte dit ce qu'on s'engage à faire pour ces gens-là. Elle est écrite avant le chantier, pour pouvoir nous être opposée pendant.

### Pourquoi on commence par les gens

Un parc de recharge couvert de panneaux solaires, c'est facile à défendre sur le plan écologique. Un peu trop facile, d'ailleurs. L'électricité ontarienne est déjà très peu carbonée : dire que notre site « décarbone la mobilité » reviendrait à s'attribuer le travail du réseau. Ce qui nous revient vraiment, c'est le béton qu'on coule et la façon dont on fera tourner le site pendant trente ans. Au conseil municipal comme au café d'en face, on nous demandera qui va travailler ici, si les entreprises du coin auront leur chance, et si les communautés autochtones ont été prévenues avant ou après les permis.

### Les cinq engagements

1. **Construire ici, embaucher ici** — Emploi local mesuré, apprentis parrainés, entente de retombées communautaires signée avant le premier coup de pelle.
2. **La relation d'abord, le contrat ensuite** — Communautés autochtones engagées avant le dépôt des permis. Reconnaissance territoriale à chaque étape publique, 30 septembre chômé sur le site.
3. **Un salaire affiché, un parcours reconnu** — Fourchette de rémunération dans chaque annonce, aucune exigence d'expérience canadienne, réponse sous 21 jours là où la loi en autorise 45.
4. **Un site où personne ne reste à la porte** — Accessibilité décidée sur les plans : emplacements, bornes, cheminements, interfaces conformes WCAG 2.0 AA.
5. **Ce qu'on achète nous engage aussi** — Clause travail forcé et travail des enfants dans 100 % des contrats de biens, part d'achats inclusifs publiée.

### Les chiffres qu'on se donne

| Objectif | Échéance |
| --- | --- |
| 40 % des heures de chantier travaillées par des gens de la municipalité d'accueil ou limitrophe | Fin du chantier |
| 8 apprentis parrainés | Fin du chantier |

| 5 % de la valeur des contrats à des entreprises autochtones vérifiées | 1er exercice complet |
| 15 % de la valeur des contrats à des fournisseurs locaux, autochtones ou certifiés divers | 1er exercice complet |

| Zéro accident avec arrêt de travail | Chaque année |
| Rapport social publié dans les quatre mois suivant la clôture | Chaque année |

PAIR pour les relations autochtones, vérifiées par un jury autochtone · Défi 50-30 du gouvernement du Canada · certification CAMSC de nos fournisseurs · rapport d'accessibilité déposé au portail provincial · certification santé-sécurité exigée de chaque entreprise intervenante.
